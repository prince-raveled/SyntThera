How SynThera works, from raw data to a live demo

A plain-language walk through everything we built: the data, the AI model, what kind of machine learning is involved, the pipeline, the website, and how to run the site in front of judges. Every number here comes from the project's own result files.

The short version

SynThera looks for approved drugs that switch genes on and off the way a workout does. At its heart it is a ranking engine: it holds up one fingerprint (exercise) against 29,680 drug fingerprints and asks which drugs look most alike.

1 · Exercise fingerprint

From real people in the MoTrPAC study: which genes went up or down after one workout, in muscle, blood and fat, compared with people who rested.

2 · Drug fingerprints

From LINCS L1000: which genes went up or down when human cells were treated with each of 29,680 compounds.

3 · Matching

Four methods score every drug, one of them an AI model (GSFM). The votes are merged, the stress part of exercise is separated from the good part, and approved, non-toxic drugs at the top become the shortlist.

The result: 82 candidate drugs for endurance exercise (cycling) and 77 for resistance exercise (weights), ready for lab testing.

If someone asks you to explain it in one breath

"SynThera is an unsupervised similarity-ranking engine, a connectivity map. It scores 29,680 compounds against human exercise gene signatures with four methods, including the GSFM gene-set foundation model, merges them with Robust Rank Aggregation, and keeps approved drugs that copy exercise's adaptations rather than its stress."

What kind of machine learning is this?

It is not a tree model, and it is not a model trained to predict something. There is no train/test split and no accuracy score, and that is on purpose.

The closest family you already know is distance-based. Think of k-nearest neighbours: kNN finds the points closest to a new point. SynThera finds the drugs closest to exercise. The difference is what happens next. kNN uses the neighbours to vote on a label. For us, the ranked list of nearest drugs is the answer.

Why not train a classifier?

A classifier needs labelled examples: many drugs already known to mimic exercise and many known not to. That list barely exists. There are a handful of proposed "exercise pills" (AICAR, GW-501516, metformin, resveratrol), most discovered in mice or rats, against 29,680 compounds. A model trained on four positives would memorise them and tell us nothing new.

Connectivity mapping needs no labels, only the two kinds of fingerprints. It is the standard method for finding new uses for drugs from gene expression (the Connectivity Map, Lamb et al., Science 2006; LINCS L1000, Subramanian et al., Cell 2017). Instead of a test set, we check the result with controls: drugs whose effect we already know (see How we know it works).

So where is the AI?

In two places. GSFM, a pretrained neural network, is one of the four scorers. Using a model someone else trained on a much bigger dataset is called transfer learning, the same idea as using a pretrained image model to get image features. And the overall design is an ensemble: four different scorers vote, so no single method decides.

GSFM, explained from zero

GSFM stands for Gene Set Foundation Model. It was built by the Ma'ayan Lab at Mount Sinai (Clarke, Marino and Ma'ayan, Patterns 2026) and is published on Hugging Face as maayanlab/gsfm-rummagene. "Foundation" means the same as in "foundation language model": trained once on a large body of data so that other people can reuse it.

It is not a chatbot and does not read text. It does one thing: it turns a list of genes into 256 numbers that describe what the list is about.

What it learned from

Rummagene, a large collection of gene sets that the same lab mined from the supplementary tables of published papers. A gene set is just a list of genes a study reported together, for example "genes that rose in muscle after training". Across a huge number of such lists, genes that work together keep turning up together. That is the pattern GSFM learns.

How it is built

Layer sizes below were read from the downloaded model file.

Bar heights are drawn for shape, not to scale. 15.26 million parameters, a 61 MB file.
  • Input: 19,589 slots, one per gene in its vocabulary. A gene list becomes a row of 1s (gene is in the list) and 0s (it is not). This is called multi-hot encoding.
  • Encoder: 19,589 → 512 → 256. Each arrow is a fully connected layer with a ReLU activation, and 20% dropout during training.
  • Embedding: the 256 numbers in the middle. This is the model's compressed summary of the gene list, and the only part we use.
  • Decoder: 256 → 256 → 19,589. It tries to rebuild the original list from the 256 numbers. It is only needed while training.

This design is an autoencoder: a network trained to squeeze its input through a narrow middle and rebuild it on the other side. It needs no labels, which is called self-supervised learning. To rebuild a gene list from only 256 numbers, the model has to learn which genes travel together. So two lists about the same biology land close together even when they share few exact genes. The authors describe it as a denoising autoencoder.

Analogy: word embeddings give "doctor" and "nurse" similar vectors because the words appear in similar sentences. GSFM does the same for genes and gene lists.

How we used it

  1. For every drug, take its 100 most-raised and 100 most-lowered genes and embed each list. That gives two vectors of 256 numbers per drug, 59,360 embeddings in total. This was done once and saved (gsfm_drug_up.npy, gsfm_drug_dn.npy in data/processed).
  2. For each exercise fingerprint, embed its up list and its down list (up to 150 genes each).
  3. Subtract the average drug embedding from every vector (centering), so the score measures what is specific to each list rather than what all lists share.
  4. Score = similarity(drug up, exercise up) + similarity(drug down, exercise down) − similarity(drug up, exercise down) − similarity(drug down, exercise up).

Similarity here is cosine similarity: 1 means the two vectors point the same way, 0 means unrelated, −1 means opposite. A high score means the drug raises what exercise raises and lowers what exercise lowers, judged by biological meaning rather than exact gene names. 97.1% of the L1000 genes are in GSFM's vocabulary, so almost nothing is lost.

The same embeddings also draw the drug map on the website: UMAP places all 29,680 drugs in 2-D so that drugs with similar effects sit near each other.

The feature choices that matter

  • Feature selection: only genes present in both datasets are compared: 8,975 genes for muscle and blood, 9,541 for fat.
  • Query size: up to 150 genes per direction (the CMap convention is 10–150). If fewer than 15 genes are significant in either direction, the fingerprint falls back to the top 50 by size of change and is marked weak.
  • Drug lists: each drug's 100 most-raised and 100 most-lowered genes, used by XSum and GSFM.
  • Scaling: there is no normal feature scaling. The fairness step is the null standardisation: each drug is compared with its own scores against 200 random fingerprints (explained below).
  • Metadata are filters, not inputs: approval, mechanism and number of cell lines are applied after scoring, so they cannot bias the scores.

The pipeline, step by step

Ten Python scripts in pipeline/, run in order. Shared code lives in src/tejas/: config.py holds every path and parameter, scoring.py the four methods and the rank merge, gsfm_embed.py the GSFM wrapper, and candidates.py the shortlist rule.

    Build the exercise fingerprints 01_build_signatures.py

    Reads the three MoTrPAC tables and makes 18 fingerprints: a t-value per gene for each exercise × tissue × time.

    MoTrPAC tables → signatures_long.parquet, signature_summary.csv
  2. 02

    One fingerprint per drug 02_lincs_consensus.py

    Reads the 33 GB file once, after checking it is intact (file size plus the first, last and 20 random signatures). Keeps exemplar experiments and the 10,174 reliable genes, merges each compound's experiments with MODZ, and adds approval and mechanism from the Repurposing Hub. The only step that needs the 33 GB file.

    33 GB .gctx (720,216 signatures) → lincs_consensus_z.npy (29,680 × 10,174), compounds.parquet
  3. 03

    Sorts genes into the good adaptations of exercise, its stress response, and an ambiguous group that is left out.

    Hallmark + Reactome → gene_class.parquet (701 / 872 / 362)
  4. 04

    The scoring engine 04_score.py

    Scores every drug against every fingerprint with four methods, makes the scores fair, merges them, tests significance and computes the benefit index. About 29 minutes on a 6-core laptop. Detailed in the next section.

    fingerprints + drug matrix + GSFM → scores_long.parquet, overall.parquet
  5. 05

    Validation 05_validate.py

    Positive and negative control drugs, permutation summaries, and how much the methods and fingerprints agree.

    → validation.json, figures
  6. 06

    Leave-one-method-out tests, a noise test with 30 reruns per fingerprint, and the UMAP drug map from GSFM embeddings.

    → robustness.json
  7. 07

    Rat comparison 07_rat_comparison.py

    Runs the identical scoring on rat training data, after converting rat genes to their human equivalents.

    → rat_comparison.json
  8. 08

    Writes the candidate lists, ranking tables and a JSON-LD file (schema.org / Bioschemas) with provenance and licences, so other tools can find and reuse the results.

The four scorers

WTCS

Classic CMap score

Sort the drug's genes from most raised to most lowered and walk down the list. Do exercise's up genes bunch at the top and its down genes at the bottom? A weighted Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic measures the bunching. This is the method of the original Connectivity Map; our fast version matches the slow textbook version to within 3×10⁻⁷.

Spearman

Rank correlation

Rank the 978 directly measured "landmark" genes by how much exercise changed them, then by how much the drug changed them. Do the two orders agree?

XSum

Extreme genes

Take the drug's 100 most-raised genes and add up exercise's t-values for them. Do the same for its 100 most-lowered genes and subtract.

GSFM

AI model

Embed the gene lists of both and compare their meaning with cosine similarity, as described in GSFM from zero.

For every method a positive score means "the drug moves genes the way exercise does" and a negative score means "the drug does the opposite".

Making scores fair: null standardisation

Some compounds change thousands of genes strongly, so they score high against almost anything. To stop them winning everywhere, we build 200 fake exercise fingerprints for each real one by shuffling which gene got which t-value. Every drug is scored against all of them. Each real score is then expressed as "how many standard deviations above this drug's own random scores". A drug that matches everything gets no credit for matching exercise.

Scale: 29,680 drugs × 18 fingerprints × 201 versions (1 real + 200 random) × 4 methods ≈ 430 million scores. That is why step 04 takes about half an hour.

Merging the four votes: Robust Rank Aggregation

Each method gives every drug a rank. RRA (Kolde et al., 2012) looks at a drug's four ranks and asks: if these ranks were random, how unlikely is it to rank this well this consistently? It computes that probability with the beta distribution and multiplies by 4 to account for looking at four methods. The result is called ρ (rho); smaller is better. Ties are broken by the average of the four ranks. "Robust" means one method's odd vote cannot drag a drug up or down on its own.

For significance, the same merge is run on the 200 random fingerprints. Comparing a drug's real ρ with that pool gives its p-value, which is then corrected for testing 29,680 drugs with the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR. A second RRA across the 10 informative fingerprints gives each drug one overall rank for cycling and one for weights.

Good match or just stress? The benefit index

The first plain ranking exposed a problem: 8 of the top 15 matches for cycling were cell-killing drugs. A hard workout and a toxic drug both set off the same alarm genes in cells. So genes are sorted into three modules:

Adaptive
701

Oxidative phosphorylation, fatty-acid metabolism, peroxisome, muscle growth, new blood vessels, mTORC1, mitochondrial biogenesis, the PGC-1α programme

Stress
872

TNF-α / NF-κB, inflammation, IL-6 / JAK / STAT3, interferon-γ, complement, apoptosis, p53, unfolded protein response

Ambiguous, left out
362

Hypoxia, reactive oxygen species, and immediate-early genes such as FOS, JUN, EGR1, ATF3 and NR4A1

For each drug, its agreement with exercise on the adaptive genes and on the stress genes is turned into a z-score. Benefit index = adaptive z − stress z. A positive value means the drug copies more of exercise's good side than its stress.

The candidate rule

  1. Approved: clinical phase "Launched" in the Repurposing Hub.
  2. Strong match: in the top 5% of all 29,680 compounds overall, about the top 1,484.
  3. Not cell-killing: mechanism is not tubulin, proteasome, topoisomerase, alkylating, HDAC, PKC-activator, cardiac glycoside or a similar generic-stress class.
  4. Enough evidence: tested in at least 3 cell lines.

The survivors are ranked by mean benefit index, giving 82 candidates for cycling and 77 for weights. Drugs that fail a rule stay in the tables with a flag rather than being silently deleted.

Limits to say out loud

  • The human data cover one exercise session, not months of training. The 12-week MoTrPAC arm was halted by COVID-19 and is not public.
  • LINCS profiles come from cancer cell lines, not muscle. Candidates must be tested in human muscle cells.
  • The rat comparison mixes species with weeks-versus-one-session effects. Against the human 24 h fingerprint the correlation is 0.21.
  • Fat and 24 h blood fingerprints are weak and exploratory.
  • Outputs are hypotheses for lab testing, not treatment advice.

How the website was built

The whole site is one HTML file, website/index.html (6.3 MB). It contains the page, its code and every result it shows. There is no framework, no server and no database.

How it is generated

  1. website/template.html (about 155 KB) holds the design and code, with an empty placeholder where the data goes: const DATA = /*__DATA__*/null;
  2. Step 09 (pipeline/09_export_website.py) reads the result files and chooses what to include: every approved drug, the top 100 for each fingerprint, the top 100 overall and the control drugs. It rounds numbers to keep the file small.
  3. It writes that data as website/data/results.json (6.1 MB) and pastes the same JSON into the placeholder, producing website/index.html.

If the science changes, re-run step 09 and the site updates. Nobody edits numbers in the page by hand.

Rounding detail: the row shows −0.5 while −0.33 − 0.11 = −0.44. The exact value is −0.446; the row rounds it twice (to −0.45, then to one decimal).

Four checks for any drug

  1. Match: top 5% or better?
  2. Confidence: High or Good?
  3. Good vs stress: positive, ideally well above +0.5?
  4. Grid: several dark cells across the muscle and blood fingerprints, not just one?

Aspirin fails all four. Sitagliptin passes them, which is why it is candidate #1.

What changes when you pick a different time

Each box is a different gene list, so choosing 15 min or 24 h instead of 3.5–4 h asks the whole question again against a new fingerprint. Nothing is recalculated in the browser: step 04 already scored all 29,680 compounds against all 18 fingerprints, and the page switches to that fingerprint's saved results. Switching Cycling ↔ Weights or Muscle ↔ Blood ↔ Fat works the same way.

The stress trap, live on the site

Pick Cycling · Muscle · 15 min or 3.5–4 h, keep "Approved only" on, and look at the top of the list: ingenol, vincristine, vinorelbine and vinblastine. Vincristine, vinorelbine and vinblastine are cancer drugs that block tubulin and kill dividing cells; ingenol activates PKC. The site tags them "cell-stress drug". They match the workout because cell damage sets off the same alarm genes. The candidate rule removes exactly these mechanisms, which is why the shortlist starts with sitagliptin, the fourth approved drug on the 3.5 h list. At 24 h the top of the list is completely different (phensuximide, propylthiouracil, topiramate). This makes a good live demo of why the benefit index exists.

Questions judges may ask

Is this a trained model? What is its accuracy?
No supervised model is trained, so there is no accuracy score. It is a similarity-ranking engine (a connectivity map), because almost no labelled exercise-mimicking drugs exist. We validate with known controls instead: mTOR blockers come out as anti-exercise in the top 0.4% (p = 5×10⁻⁵), and the top lists hold up when 20% of the data is scrambled (48–77% Jaccard overlap versus about 2% by chance).
What AI did you use?
GSFM, the Gene Set Foundation Model from the Ma'ayan Lab (2026): a 15-million-parameter autoencoder that turns gene lists into 256-number embeddings. It is one of four scorers. Removing it reshuffles the top 50 more than removing any other method: about 12 of the 50 drugs are replaced (Jaccard overlap 0.62).
Did you train GSFM yourselves?
No. We used the published weights, which is transfer learning. Our question doesn't need retraining; it needs a model that already knows which genes work together.
Why four methods instead of one?
Each one sees a different side of the match: whole ranked lists, rank order, extreme genes and biological meaning. Their rankings correlate 0.46–0.65, so they agree without being redundant. Robust Rank Aggregation rewards drugs that rank well consistently.
What is new compared with earlier work?
Earlier screens, including a 2026 Bio-IT World hackathon project that used the same GSFM model, started from rat exercise data translated to human genes. We start from human data, and we measured that the species changes the answer almost completely: 0 of the top 100 shared. The benefit index, which separates exercise's adaptations from its stress, is the second new piece.
Why did cancer drugs show up at first?
Cell damage in cancer cell lines triggers the same alarm genes as a hard workout; 8 of the top 15 plain matches for cycling were cell-killing drugs. The benefit index and the mechanism flags remove them.
Why use cancer cell lines?
That is what LINCS has at this scale: 720,216 profiles. It is a stated limitation, and the next step is testing the candidates in human muscle cells.
What hardware did it need?
A 6-core laptop. Scoring took about 29 minutes. The website is static and runs in any modern browser.
Is it reproducible?
Yes. Fixed data releases, seed 19 for every random step, all intermediate files saved, and a FAIR JSON-LD export with provenance and licences.
Could people take these drugs instead of exercising?
No. These are leads for lab testing, not treatment advice.

Glossary

Signature / fingerprint
A list of genes with how strongly each went up or down.
t-statistic
A signed number for how strongly a gene changed relative to its noise. Positive = up, negative = down.
FDR
False discovery rate: the expected share of false hits among the results you call significant. We use 0.05.
Connectivity map
The approach of matching a disease or condition fingerprint against drug fingerprints to find drugs that copy or reverse it.
L1000
The LINCS lab method that measures 978 "landmark" genes directly and infers the rest.
MODZ
A weighted average of a drug's replicate fingerprints that gives more weight to the consistent ones.
Embedding
A short list of numbers (here 256) that a neural network uses to summarise something bigger.
Autoencoder
A neural network trained to compress its input and rebuild it, which forces it to learn the important patterns.
Cosine similarity
How closely two vectors point the same way: 1 same, 0 unrelated, −1 opposite.
Null / permutation
Scores from random, shuffled inputs, used to see what "by chance" looks like.
RRA
Robust Rank Aggregation: merges several rankings and asks whether an item ranks better than chance across them.
Benefit index
Adaptive-gene agreement minus stress-gene agreement.
Jaccard overlap
Shared items divided by all distinct items in two lists. 1 = identical, 0 = nothing shared.
UMAP
A method that squeezes many numbers per item into 2-D for a map, keeping similar items close.
FAIR / JSON-LD
Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable data, written in a standard machine-readable format.
